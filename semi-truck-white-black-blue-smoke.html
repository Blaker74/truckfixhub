☁️ White smoke / vapor

Think about temperature, combustion, fuel and coolant. White exhaust on a cold start can mean something different from persistent white exhaust after warm-up.

If coolant is disappearing too, follow the coolant-loss path. If the engine is hard to start cold or runs rough, follow those clues too.

💧 White smoke + coolant loss❄️ Hard cold start

🌑 Black smoke

Think fuel being burned without enough usable air—but do not assume the turbo is bad. Intake restriction, charge-air leakage, boost/control issues, fuel delivery and sensor/control problems can overlap.

🌀 Low-boost picture⛽ Fuel-side power

🔵 Blue or blue-gray smoke

Think about engine oil reaching the combustion or exhaust stream. Record oil consumption and when the smoke appears. Turbo oil leakage, crankcase ventilation and internal engine conditions are possibilities—not conclusions.

Blue smoke alone does not prove the turbo or engine needs replacement.

🧒 Smoke diagnosis made simple

1️⃣ COLOR
2️⃣ WHEN
3️⃣ WHAT ELSE?
4️⃣ TEST

Record smoke color, cold/hot condition, idle/load, oil or coolant use, loss of power, rough running, codes and recent repairs. Those clues together are far stronger than color alone.

SEE IT → NAME IT → TEST IT → PROVE IT.

Smoke color is evidence, not a parts order. Lighting, temperature, regeneration and operating conditions can change how exhaust looks. Combine the color with the timing and the truck's other symptoms before testing the likely system.

🛑 SAFETY: Stop safely for heavy smoke with a red stop-engine warning, rapid oil or coolant loss, severe overheating, abnormal mechanical noise, uncontrolled engine speed, or another manufacturer-directed shutdown condition. Verify exact diagnostic procedures for the exact engine.
