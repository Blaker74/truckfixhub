TRUCKFIXHUB ENGINE SYMPTOM GUIDE

Semi Truck Blowing White, Black or Blue Smoke?

Smoke color is a clue—not a complete diagnosis. Record when the smoke appears, engine temperature, load, fluid consumption and fault codes before deciding which system has failed.

Stop if the symptoms are severe

If heavy smoke is accompanied by a red stop-engine warning, rapid oil or coolant loss, severe overheating, abnormal mechanical noise, uncontrolled engine speed or another manufacturer-directed shutdown condition, stop safely and follow OEM procedures.

White smoke or vapor

White exhaust can have different causes depending on temperature and operating conditions. Technicians may investigate incomplete combustion, fuel-system concerns, cold-engine operation, coolant entering the combustion/exhaust path, or other engine-specific conditions. Persistent white smoke with unexplained coolant loss deserves prompt diagnosis.

Black smoke

Black smoke can indicate that combustion has more fuel than available air can properly support. Areas of investigation can include air restriction, charge-air leaks, boost/turbo performance, fuel delivery or injection concerns, sensor data and engine-control conditions. Modern emissions-equipped trucks may behave differently than older mechanical diesels.

Blue or blue-gray smoke

Blue-toned smoke can be associated with engine oil entering the combustion or exhaust stream. Oil consumption, turbocharger-related oil leakage, crankcase ventilation concerns and internal engine conditions may be investigated. Smoke color alone does not prove the turbocharger or engine needs replacement.

When does the smoke happen?

  • Only during cold start
  • Only under acceleration or heavy load
  • After extended idling
  • During or around regeneration events
  • After the engine reaches operating temperature
  • Constantly at idle and while driving

The timing of the symptom can be as important as the color.

What to record for the technician

  • Smoke color and whether it has a noticeable odor
  • Cold, hot, idle, acceleration or heavy-load conditions
  • Oil or coolant consumption
  • Loss of power or low boost
  • Engine temperature
  • Check-engine light and SPN/FMI fault information
  • Recent turbo, injector, EGR, aftertreatment or engine repairs

Smoke plus loss of power

If unusual smoke appears with low power on hills, the combination may provide useful diagnostic direction. Fuel, air, boost, aftertreatment and engine condition all remain possibilities until tested.

Smoke plus rough idle or engine shaking

If smoke appears with a rough idle, misfire-like symptom or engine vibration, record whether it happens only cold, after warm-up, at idle or under load. That pattern can help a technician separate fuel, air, electrical and mechanical possibilities.

Smoke plus check-engine light

Record the SPN/FMI or manufacturer fault information before clearing codes. The fault information and smoke pattern together are more useful than either clue by itself.

Check engine light guide →

Do not diagnose from color alone

Lighting, temperature, regeneration and operating conditions can affect how exhaust appears. TruckFixHub provides educational troubleshooting information. Confirm the cause using the correct engine manufacturer's diagnostic procedures before replacing injectors, turbochargers, EGR components or internal engine parts.
