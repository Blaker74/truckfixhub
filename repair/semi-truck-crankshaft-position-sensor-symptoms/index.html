Semi-Truck Crankshaft Position Sensor Symptoms

The crankshaft position or engine-speed sensor provides information the engine control system uses to determine crankshaft speed and position. A missing or unreliable signal can contribute to a crank-no-start, stalling or intermittent shutdown condition.

Symptoms alone do not prove the sensor has failed. Wiring, connectors, sensor supply circuits, the target or tone wheel and ECM inputs can produce similar results.

Possible Symptoms

  • Engine cranks but will not start
  • No engine RPM shown in diagnostic data while cranking
  • Intermittent stall or shutdown
  • Hard starting
  • Engine-speed or position-related fault codes
  • Loss of synchronization between crankshaft and camshaft signals
  • Problem changes with engine temperature or vibration

Diagnostic Order

  1. Read active and inactive fault codes.
  2. Verify ECM communication and ECM power/grounds.
  3. Watch engine RPM or crankshaft-speed data during cranking.
  4. Check crank/cam synchronization data if supported.
  5. Inspect the sensor connector and harness.
  6. Verify sensor power, ground or circuit integrity as applicable to the sensor design.
  7. Test the sensor signal using OEM procedures and appropriate equipment.
  8. Inspect the sensor mounting, air gap where applicable, and target/tone wheel condition.
  9. Investigate intermittent heat, vibration or harness-related faults.
  10. Replace the sensor only after the circuit and mechanical trigger have been evaluated.

Zero RPM While Cranking

If diagnostic data shows no engine-speed signal during a normal crank attempt, the crankshaft-speed circuit deserves immediate attention. However, zero RPM does not by itself prove the sensor is defective.

Check the Wiring First

Inspect for rubbed-through harnesses, oil or coolant contamination, loose terminals, corrosion, poor connector retention and damage near the engine. Pay special attention to areas affected by recent engine or transmission work.

Sensor Types Vary

Heavy-duty engines use different sensor designs and circuits. Some sensors require a supplied voltage and ground; others generate a signal differently. Do not apply a universal resistance, voltage or air-gap specification. Use the engine manufacturer's service information.

Crank and Cam Synchronization

Some engine-management systems compare crankshaft and camshaft signals. A synchronization problem can involve either sensor, either circuit, timing-related mechanical issues or the trigger components. Use available live data and OEM diagnostic trees.

Intermittent Failure

A truck that starts cold but stalls hot, or restarts after cooling, may have an intermittent electrical or sensor problem. Capture fault codes and live data during the failure when possible rather than replacing parts based only on the pattern.

Before Replacing the Sensor

Verify the ECM is powered and communicating, confirm the symptom in live data, inspect the connector and harness, test the circuit, and inspect the trigger/tone-wheel system where accessible.

FAQ

Can a bad crankshaft position sensor cause a diesel engine to crank but not start?

Yes. The ECM may be unable to determine engine speed or position, but other circuit and mechanical faults can create the same symptom.

Does zero RPM while cranking prove the crankshaft sensor is bad?

No. It is a strong diagnostic clue, not final proof. Test the complete circuit and related components.

Can a crankshaft position sensor fail intermittently?

Yes. Heat, vibration, wiring and connector problems can make the signal intermittent.
