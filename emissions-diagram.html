♻️ SEMI TRUCK WON'T REGEN / DERATED?

Read the faults first. The derate is often the result—not the root cause.

Quick answer

If a diesel truck will not complete a regeneration, do not keep forcing parked regens without finding out why. An active engine or aftertreatment fault, implausible temperature or pressure data, excessive soot production, insufficient exhaust temperature, DPF restriction or a DEF/SCR problem can prevent successful regeneration or contribute to a derate. Save the fault codes and operating data first, then diagnose the earliest cause you can prove.

👇 PICK THE COMPLAINT

🌫️ It will not regen

  1. Read and save every active fault before forcing another regeneration attempt.
  2. Check whether an engine or aftertreatment fault is preventing regeneration.
  3. Review soot/load information, differential pressure and exhaust-temperature readings for plausibility.
  4. Verify the conditions required by the manufacturer for a parked or commanded regen are actually met.
  5. If regen starts but cannot complete, diagnose why temperature, flow or system performance is not reaching the required condition.

🐌 It is derated / losing power

  1. Record all active and inactive fault codes before clearing anything.
  2. Identify the earliest or root fault instead of chasing only the derate message.
  3. Check for engine air/fuel faults that may be creating excessive soot or poor exhaust conditions.
  4. Then evaluate DPF restriction, sensor plausibility, DEF/SCR operation and related wiring as directed by the fault path.
  5. Repair the proven cause and follow the manufacturer procedure for verification and derate recovery.

💧 DEF / NOx warning keeps coming back

  1. Write down the complete fault code and description.
  2. For DEF faults, verify fluid level/quality and inspect dosing, pressure, heaters and lines as applicable.
  3. For NOx faults, inspect wiring and connectors and compare sensor information with overall system conditions.
  4. Remember that an upstream engine, exhaust-leak or aftertreatment problem can make downstream readings look wrong.
  5. Do not replace a NOx sensor, doser or SCR component just because its name appears in a fault description.

📚 DPF soot vs. ash — the simple version

A DPF can contain both soot and ash, but they are not the same problem. Understanding that difference prevents a lot of wasted regen attempts and unnecessary parts replacement.

🔥 SOOT can be burned

Soot is combustible particulate produced by the engine. The aftertreatment system uses regeneration to raise temperature and oxidize accumulated soot when the required operating conditions are met. If soot keeps building faster than the system can manage it, the real question is why: excessive soot production, a condition preventing regeneration, bad information from a sensor, insufficient temperature, or another engine/aftertreatment fault may be involved.

🪨 ASH does not burn away in a regen

Ash is non-combustible material that accumulates in the filter over time. A successful regeneration does not make accumulated ash disappear. When the applicable manufacturer maintenance interval or diagnostic procedure calls for DPF ash service, the filter requires the approved physical cleaning/service process rather than another attempt to burn it out.

🔁 Why repeated parked regens can be the wrong move

A parked regen is a procedure, not a universal repair. If a fault is preventing regeneration, the engine is producing abnormal soot, the system cannot develop the required temperature, sensor data is implausible, or the filter's problem is ash rather than soot, repeatedly commanding another regen does not correct the underlying cause.

🧠 What TruckFixHub wants you to remember

Before condemning the DPF, identify the engine and emissions generation, save all active faults, determine whether the concern is soot loading or scheduled ash service, and diagnose the reason the system cannot manage the load. Exact thresholds, service intervals and procedures vary by manufacturer and generation, so verify those against the current OEM information for the truck.

Bottom line: a regen complaint does not automatically mean “bad DPF,” and a DPF service interval does not automatically mean the engine has a regen failure. Diagnose which problem you actually have.

Aftertreatment faults can be connected

A problem upstream can create symptoms downstream. Engine faults can affect soot production and exhaust temperature; inaccurate temperature or pressure information can affect regeneration decisions; DEF/SCR faults can create NOx-related complaints. Record all active and inactive codes and operating data, then follow the manufacturer’s diagnostic order instead of replacing the component named in the last code.
