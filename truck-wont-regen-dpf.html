DPF · PARKED REGEN · AFTERTREATMENT

Truck won't regen?

If a parked DPF regeneration will not start—or starts but will not complete—don't automatically condemn the DPF. First determine whether the regen is being inhibited, failing to achieve the required conditions, or being requested repeatedly because another problem remains.

Quick answer: why won't a parked regen start?

A heavy-duty truck may refuse a parked regen when required enable conditions are not satisfied or when an active fault prevents regeneration. The exact logic varies by engine and vehicle, but useful first checks include:

  • Record all active and inactive SPN/FMI faults before clearing anything.
  • Confirm the engine meets the OEM temperature requirements.
  • Verify fuel level and other manufacturer-specific regen enable conditions.
  • Confirm parking brake, transmission/neutral and applicable safety interlocks.
  • Look for active engine or aftertreatment faults that can inhibit regeneration.
  • Review reported soot load, differential pressure and recent regeneration history with compatible diagnostic software.

Parked regen won't start vs. regen won't complete

Won't start: concentrate first on enable conditions, active faults and whether the control system is permitting regeneration.

Starts but won't complete: the diagnostic path can move toward temperature performance, sensing, dosing, DPF restriction/condition or another fault that interrupts the event. Use the exact OEM diagnostic procedure for the engine and aftertreatment system.

DPF differential pressure matters—but don't guess from the code

The system uses pressure information to help evaluate exhaust restriction and aftertreatment conditions. A pressure-related fault can involve the sensor, tubes/hoses, wiring, plausibility or an actual restriction depending on the system. Inspect and test before replacing the sensor or DPF.

Why does the truck keep asking for a regen?

Repeated regen requests are a reason to diagnose the cause, not simply keep performing regenerations. Depending on the engine and fault set, areas may include excessive engine-out soot, exhaust-temperature performance, differential-pressure sensing, dosing, aftertreatment condition and vehicle duty cycle.

Can a bad NOx sensor stop a regen?

Aftertreatment faults can interact with regeneration and inducement strategies, but a NOx-related code does not prove the sensor itself is defective. Use the complete fault set and OEM diagnostic logic to determine whether the sensor, wiring, exhaust condition or another system issue is responsible.

Do not force a regen blindly

High soot load, active faults, fuel/exhaust concerns or damaged aftertreatment components can require OEM-directed testing or service before a regeneration is appropriate. Follow the engine manufacturer's procedures. Do not bypass emissions controls or repeatedly clear faults to force operation.

Before replacing the DPF

A failed regeneration by itself does not prove the DPF requires replacement. Record the fault set, soot/load information, differential-pressure data, regeneration history and the conditions under which the regen failed. That evidence gives a technician a much better starting point.
